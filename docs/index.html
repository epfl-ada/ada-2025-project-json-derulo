The Premise

"A day without laughter is a day wasted," Charlie Chaplin once said. We say: a day analyzing 2.2 million New Yorker captions is a day spent finding out why your joke didn't win.

In recent years, the Internet culture has prioritized shorter and trending jokes. Tweets (X's?) and TikToks are defining a new era of "blink and you'll miss the punchline." We dove into the New Yorker's dataset to see if these trends hold up in the high-brow world of cartoon captions.

We analyzed over 2,292,678 captions to find the secret formula of comedy.
The Result: Everyone is Shakespeare. Or everyone is a five-year-old. The average TTR was 0.99 across the board. There is effectively no difference in vocabulary richness between the winners and the losers.

"Using sesquipedalian verbiage does not augment comedic efficacy."

Translation: Big words don't make you funny.

Lesson 3: The Ghost in the Machine

BLABLABLA. Using Spherical k-means clustering and c-TF-IDF, we sought hidden themes.

We found that captions tend to cluster around specific topics depending on the image (groundbreaking, we know). But teaching a computer to understand why a man in a chicken suit is funny remains the ultimate punchline.

What We Learned (The Hard Way)

Science is about trial and error. Mostly error.

  • Classifying Humor Types: We tried to categorize jokes into "Irony", "Dark Humor", and "Wordplay". Turns out, explaining a joke to an algorithm kills both the joke and the algorithm. We decided to pivot faster than a startup in 2021.
  • Context Analysis: We attempted to correlate humor with world events using the GDELT dataset. Then we discovered the dataset was 288GB. We decided that political stability is important, but preserving our RAM is importanter.

The Verdict

So, how does one win the New Yorker Caption Contest?

  1. Keep it short. (Around 10 words is the sweet spot).
  2. Keep it simple. Don't overthink the vocabulary.
  3. Be lucky. (We didn't test for this, but it helps).
"Analysis kills spontaneity. That's why we did it."

To Go Further

Our comedy career might be ending here, but the science goes on. If we had more time (and more RAM), we would:

  • Develop a "Funniness Score" Predictor: Train a model to rate your caption before you submit it, saving you from public embarrassment.
  • Analyze Visual Complexity: Does a simple drawing need a complex caption, or vice versa?
  • Finally crack the GDELT dataset: Maybe next year, when hard drives are bigger.
