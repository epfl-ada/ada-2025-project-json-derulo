How to be funny, for dummies

A data-driven guide to cracking the New Yorker Caption Contest.
(Or at least, how not to fail miserably.)

Start Laughing

The Challenge

"A day without laughter is a day wasted." - Charlie Chaplin.
"A day analyzing 2.2 million New Yorker captions is a day... well, it's a day." - Team JSON Derulo.

We dove headfirst into the New Yorker Caption Contest dataset. That's hundreds of contests, thousands of images, and millions of people trying to be funny. We asked the big questions: What makes a caption win? Is it the length? The vocabulary? The existential dread?

We analyzed over 2,292,678 captions to find the secret formula of comedy.

Lesson 1: Size Matters (Keep it Short)

You know that friend who takes 10 minutes to tell a punchline? Don't be that friend.

Our structural analysis revealed a groundbreaking truth: Brevity is the soul of wit. Okay, Shakespeare said it first, but we have p-values to prove it (p < 0.0001, baby!).

10.3 Top 20% (Funny)
11.0 Bottom 20% (Not Funny)

Average Word Count

The top-performing captions averaged 10.3 words. The bottom ones? 11.0 words. Is that a huge difference? No. Is it statistically significant? Absolutely. So, cut that extra adjective. It might cost you the contest.

Lesson 2: Don't Use Big Words

We thought, "Maybe funny people use complex vocabulary!" We calculated the Type-Token Ratio (TTR) to measure vocabulary richness.

Result: Everyone is basically Shakespeare. Or a 5-year-old. The average TTR was 0.99 across the board. There is effectively no difference in vocabulary richness between the winners and the losers.

"Using sesquipedalian verbiage does not augment comedic efficacy."
Translation: Big words don't make you funny.

Lesson 3: What Are We Joking About?

We trained an AI (BERT, our new best friend) to read the jokes so we didn't have to. We used Spherical k-means clustering and c-TF-IDF to find the hidden themes.

We found that captions tend to cluster around specific topics depending on the image (groundbreaking, we know). But teaching a computer to understand "why" a man in a chicken suit is funny? That's the real punchline.

What We Learned (The Hard Way)

Science is about trial and error. Mostly error.

  • Classifying Humor Types: We tried to categorize jokes into "Irony", "Dark Humor", and "Wordplay". Turns out, explaining a joke to an algorithm kills the joke and the algorithm. We dropped this faster than a bad stand-up set.
  • Context Analysis: We wanted to see if world events (using the GDELT dataset) influenced humor. Then we saw the dataset was 288GB. We decided that political stability is important, but not "crash our laptops" important.

Conclusion

So, how do you win the New Yorker Caption Contest?

  1. Keep it short. (Around 10 words is the sweet spot).
  2. Don't overthink the vocabulary. Simple works.
  3. Be lucky. (We didn't test for this, but it helps).
"Analysis kills spontaneity. That's why we did it."
