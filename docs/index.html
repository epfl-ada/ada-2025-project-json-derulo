The Premise

"A day without laughter is a day wasted," Charlie Chaplin once said. We say: a day analyzing 2.2 million New Yorker captions is a day spent finding out why your joke didn't win.

In recent years, the Internet culture has prioritized shorter and trending jokes. Tweets (X's?) and TikToks are defining a new era of "blink and you'll miss the punchline." We dove into the New Yorker's dataset to see if these trends hold up in the high-brow world of cartoon captions.

We analyzed over 2,292,678 captions to find the secret formula of comedy.

The Research Questions

We didn't just doom-scroll through cartoons; we had a plan. A scientific plan. (Mostly). Here is what we set out to discover:

  • Structural Analysis: Are short jokes funnier, or do we just stop reading after ten words? What about the punctuation?
  • Theme Analysis: What themes have a tendency to be ranked better? Do image and text themes need to match, or is randomness the key to success?
  • Semantic Analysis: Which humor type reigns supreme: dark humor, irony, social commentary, wordplay, or pure absurdity?
  • Temporal Analysis: Is comedy timeless, or just what happens when people have too much free time and too much confidence?

Why We Analyzed This

With millions of captions and votes, this dataset allows us to quantify humor, compare audience taste, and confirm a long-held suspicion: comedy is subjective, but not that subjective.

Lesson 1: Brevity is the Soul of Wit

You know that friend who takes ten minutes to get to the punchline? Do not be that friend. Our structural analysis revealed a groundbreaking truth: size matters, and smaller is better.

Average Word Count

The top-performing captions averaged 9 words. The bottom ones? 11.0 words. A small difference? Perhaps. But with a p-value less than 0.0001, it is statistically indisputable. Cut that extra adjective. It might cost you the contest.

Lesson 2: Less is More

We hypothesized that the funniest people might employ a complex vocabulary. To test this, we calculated the Type-Token Ratio (TTR) to measure vocabulary richness across millions of entries.

Mathematically, the TTR is expressed as:

TTR = V / N

Where V is the number of unique words (types) and N is the total number of words (tokens) in the text.

The Result: Everyone is Shakespeare. Or everyone is a five-year-old. The average TTR was 0.99 across the board. There is effectively no difference in vocabulary richness between the winners and the losers.

"Using sesquipedalian verbiage does not augment comedic efficacy."

Translation: Big words don't make you funny.

Lesson 4: Semantic Analysis

Semantics is just what words mean.
Not how fancy they sound.
Not how they’re arranged.
Just: when you say something, what are you actually talking about?

To impose some order on the famously unruly universe of caption contests, we used Berger’s humor categories—borrowed from a man who looked at comedy, thought “this needs diagrams,” and wrote an entire book about it.

The categories:

  • Language: jokes that live by words alone—irony, puns, allusions, repartee, wit, and ridicule.
  • Logic: jokes powered by ideas rather than phrasing—absurdity, coincidence, conceptual surprise, and misunderstandings.
  • Identity: jokes that turn the lens on people and personas—parody, existential humor, condescension, self-deprecation, and other small human failures.
  • Action: jokes that skip language entirely, relying on physical or non-verbal cues like awkward bodies, odd expressions, clumsiness, or general chaos.

Using Ollama and the gpt-oss:120b model, we analyzed the 25 best and 25 worst captions from each contest—a practical compromise given the computational cost. Captions could belong to multiple categories, with the model assigning percentages accordingly.

Source: An Anatomy of Humor, Arthur Asa Berger (1993)

Our results point to a humbling truth: there is no cheat code for comedy. All humor categories perform about the same, which means being funny is less about what kind of joke you tell and more about not messing it up.

One pattern does stand out. Language and Logic don’t like sharing the spotlight. Jokes usually twist words or ideas—not both. Try to be clever in two ways at once and the meaning trips over itself. Comedy, like traffic, works best when everyone stays in their lane.

Identity and Action mostly play supporting roles. A little self-awareness or physical awkwardness helps, but rarely carries the whole joke unless someone is actively falling over.

The takeaway:

  • Decide where the meaning lives.
  • In the words.
  • The idea.
  • The person.
  • Or the body.

Keep it clear, keep it simple—and remember: if the audience is busy decoding your joke, they’re not laughing at it.

Lesson 4: Timing is Everything

They say comedy is about timing. In the case of the New Yorker Caption Contest, it's about being stuck at home with nothing else to do.

We plotted the number of captions submitted over time, and the results are about as unsurprising as you'd expect.

Notice that massive spike? That's the COVID-19 pandemic. Apparently, when the world stops turning, everyone decides they are a comedian.

But look at the trend line. It’s dropping faster than our self-esteem after reading the comments.

"To win, you need to go back in time to the Prime Era (circa 2020)."

Or just wait for the next global catastrophe. (Please don't.)

What We Learned (The Hard Way)

Science is about trial and error. Mostly error.

  • Classifying Humor Types: We tried to categorize jokes into "Irony", "Dark Humor", and "Wordplay". Turns out, explaining a joke to an algorithm kills both the joke and the algorithm. We decided to pivot faster than a startup in 2021.
  • Context Analysis: We attempted to correlate humor with world events using the GDELT dataset. Then we discovered the dataset was 288GB. We decided that political stability is important, but preserving our RAM is importanter.

The Verdict

So, how does one win the New Yorker Caption Contest?

  1. Keep it short. (Around 10 words is the sweet spot).
  2. Keep it simple. Don't overthink the vocabulary.
  3. Be lucky. (We didn't test for this, but it helps).
"Analysis kills spontaneity. That's why we did it."

To Go Further

Our comedy career might be ending here, but the science goes on. If we had more time (and more RAM), we would:

  • Develop a "Funniness Score" Predictor: Train a model to rate your caption before you submit it, saving you from public embarrassment.
  • Analyze Visual Complexity: Does a simple drawing need a complex caption, or vice versa?
  • Finally crack the GDELT dataset: Maybe next year, when hard drives are bigger.
